The most telling exhibits are the photographs taken by Wehrmacht cameramen, strictly against order. They show soldiers pulling on the beards of elderly Jews, people digging their own graves, carts piled high with bodies and roadside gallows. Women and children could be defined as spies therefore shot out of hand. The exhibition is difficult to accept for many former soldiers and their descendants. In the visitor's book someone has written: »Father, where were you?« Many German veterans say they knew nothing of the crimes against Jews. The Times, 18.03.95

The exhibition is a bitter indictment of an army that is still today passed off as the »only decent organisation« in Hitler's state, spawning heroic figures such as Field Marshall Rommel and the generals who tried to assassinate the Führer. A totally different picture emerges in the exhibition, which focusses on the invasions of Yugoslavia, White Russia and the advance on Stalingrad. It reveals that the regular army operated as one of the pillars of the regime and that its officers and men engaged in genocide against Jews and gipsies. The European, 17.03.95

Here, in grainy black-and-white photographs, some taken as grisly souvenirs, ordinary German soldiers – not SS madmen – are shown humiliating Jews and conducting summary executions by firing squad and hanging. Here is a letter written home by a young soldier, boasting that his unit had killed 1.000 Jews – »and that was not enough«. The exhibit has already toured 15 cities in Germany and Austria, but only in traditionally conservative Munich, where Nazism took root in the 1920's, has it become an object of ferocious political division, to the extend that both the far right and the leftist groups called rival demonstrations. [...] Yet most remarkable of all, the exhibit has broken a wall of silence between young Germans who learned principally about the Holocaust during high school, and a much older generation that took part in the Second World War and then, by and large, took refuge in self-imposed silence.
